# IPHS 400 MP2 Report — Kenyon Men's Soccer CMS

Mays Gunderson · 2026-10-14

## 1. What I built, and which grill decisions were mine
 I built a small website and admin console for Kenyon Men’s Soccer, where student media members can log in and write game updates and pages. The public site runs on GitHub Pages and uses Kenyon’s purple and white colors. While editors can write and save drafts, only the admin can publish them. This allows students to contribute to the site while ensuring that everything is reviewed before it becomes public.

Also, I suggested adding a History page during the grill, telling Claude that the site “could also have a history section, explaining all previous titles and accomplishments of past teams.” Much of the team’s history is scattered across different sources or only known by those who were involved. Having this information in one place would give alumni and potential recruits a way to learn about the accomplishments of past teams. Unlike game updates, this information would remain largely the same throughout the season, which is why I made it a separate page in the menu.
## 2. Where the AI drifted, and what caught it
The clearest example of drift occurred in T02 (issue #3), where editors write update drafts. Claude wrote a test to check that a new update was saved as a draft, but the test only checked whether the word “Draft” appeared somewhere on the page. Since the success message already said “Draft saved,” the test would pass regardless of the update’s actual status. While the test appeared to confirm that the feature worked, it did not verify what it was meant to check. I likely would not have caught this myself, as the test was green. The /code-review step caught the issue by comparing the implementation against the spec, and the fix changed the test to look for “Status: Draft” instead. This showed me that a passing test does not necessarily confirm that a feature works correctly, as the test itself may be checking the wrong thing.
## 3. Skills, prompts, and resources I used, and why
Before designing the site, I explored WordPress Playground, which introduced me to terms such as “slug” and “draft” that I had not known before. My main workflow involved the mattpocock skills, beginning with /grill-with-docs to answer questions about what I wanted the site to do. I then used /to-spec and /to-tickets to turn those answers into a plan and seven tickets, followed by /implement and /code-review for each ticket. My most important prompt was the grill prompt, which I divided into GOAL, METHOD, and PREP (the one you provided us in class). This gave Claude instructions to interview me one question at a time so that the site would reflect what I wanted. I used Opus for building because classmates said it made fewer mistakes. As well as this, I worked in Orca as my terminal and used a separate Claude chat to explain what was happening, as I had little experience using the terminal. The most frustrating part of the process was Claude’s safety check blocking pushes, which meant I had to run them myself.
## 4. What I'd add next, and what the budget data told me
The next additions I would make are scorers and stats in match reports, as this is the information fans ask about most. As well as this, I would give editors a way to post from their own laptops, since they currently have to use the admin’s machine. The “Upcoming Fixture” page also becomes outdated after every game, which is why scheduled publishing would be a useful addition.

My budget estimates were much higher than what I actually used. I planned to use 35% of a 5-hour window for the grill, spec, and tickets, while the actual usage was about 5%. Similarly, I estimated 20% per ticket, but each ticket used about 11%. Overall, I expected to use 25–35% of my weekly cap and only used 13%. The models used for each stage were also the opposite of what I had planned. While I set opusplan for the grill, the ledger shows that Sonnet did the planning and Opus did the building. Next time, I would lower my budget estimates and deliberately use Opus for planning, as this is where the decisions about the project are made.
